How to plan home Ethernet cables and sockets before renovation

Plan your cable routes before the walls are finished. Leave room for extra cables. At handover, check that every socket is connected, labelled and tested.

Mark equipment before closing the walls

Mark the desktop, TV area and Wi-Fi points on the plan. Run each cable back to one accessible distribution location. Do not connect wall sockets in a chain.

A metal network cabinet can hold wired equipment, but it’s a poor place for a Wi-Fi router. Put the access point where it can cover the room and connect it by cable.

Run two cables to important locations

Two separate cables at a desk or TV area provide another connection and a spare. Each is a complete run back to the distribution point; do not split the wires of one cable.

Where practical, also provide accessible conduit for later expansion. Two cables and a spare route complement each other. A single cable can be a deliberate compromise when space is limited.

Do not buy Cat7 just for a higher number

Copper Cat6 with suitable sockets is a sensible starting point for a typical new home installation. For a planned 10G network, consider a complete Cat6A system. Sound existing Cat5e need not automatically be replaced.

Shielding can help in some interference environments, but needs the right installation. It does not automatically increase speed. Avoid copper-clad aluminium sold as equivalent to solid copper.

Make future cable replacement possible

Use few turns and gentle bends. Provide a pull cord and access at difficult sections. Cable fitting into a straight section does not prove it can be pulled around several tight corners.

A shared route for four to six cables needs more capacity than a branch to one outlet. Choose by actual cable thickness and conduit internal diameter. Check the route before covering it.

A fitted faceplate does not prove the socket is connected

Check that the cable is terminated in the RJ45 module and at the distribution end. Label both ends. Modules provide colour markings for the chosen wiring scheme.

T568B is a valid choice when used correctly at both ends; T568A is valid too. Test after the final socket and faceplate installation, not only before.

Accept a working link

Damaged or incorrectly terminated wires can leave only a 100 Mb/s connection, cause interruptions or prevent a link. A slow equipment port or faulty patch lead can cause similar symptoms.

A basic eight-light tester only checks some faults. Suitable measurements are needed to confirm the required performance.

  • Keep the plan and photos before routes are covered.
  • Label both ends of every run.
  • Collect test results for every completed point.

Plan a star topology with spare capacity

Each socket has its own run to a patch panel or keystone at the distribution point. For important locations, plan two independent four-pair cables, not pair splitting.

Include AP placement, wired Mesh backhaul, switch port count, power and service access. A spare route does not replace cables required at handover.

Specify the whole channel

Cat7 is unnecessary for typical home 1G/2.5G. Consider copper Cat6 for new runs and Cat6A with matching modules and patch leads when 10G over a full channel is required. Sound Cat5e can support 2.5GBASE-T.

Check conductor material, diameter/AWG and termination compatibility. Shielding is justified by EMI conditions and a correctly connected bonding system with shield continuity; it does not replace separation from power wiring.

Size the route by internal diameter

Allow for cable area, fill and bend geometry. With six 6.5 mm cables and an illustrative 40% fill, a round conduit needs about 25.2 mm internal diameter; six 8 mm cables need about 31 mm.

This is an area calculation, not a universal regulatory requirement or a pullability guarantee. Allow extra room, accessible pulling points and a pull cord. Follow the specific cable’s bend radius and pulling-tension limits.

Terminate correctly and preserve pairs

Fixed cable normally ends at a socket module and a patch panel/keystone, with patch leads to equipment. A direct plug needs compatibility with the particular conductor and installation design.

T568B defines a wiring scheme, not performance. T568A is also valid. Follow the same selected scheme at both ends and the actual IDC module markings; terminal layout can differ. Keep pair untwist minimal.

Distinguish wire map, qualification and certification

Wire map checks connections. A simple continuity test can miss a split pair: pins connect correctly but conductors do not form the intended twisted pairs. Continuity alone does not certify bandwidth or category.

A qualifier assesses support for an application; a certification analyser tests against selected channel requirements. Agree the scope first, test after final assembly and provide actual results.

Check negotiation and usable performance

1000BASE-T uses all four pairs. Some faults allow 100BASE-TX, while others cause errors or no link. Check ports and patch leads before attributing every 100 Mb/s limit to a wall socket.

2.5GBASE-T requires capable ports at both ends and a sound channel. Negotiated link rate differs from file throughput. The 100 m channel allowance includes patch leads; do not allocate the entire distance to the fixed run.

Equipment: requirements before purchases

Ordinary manufacturer links, without affiliate tracking. Check the exact hardware version; buying equipment alone does not guarantee a fix.
